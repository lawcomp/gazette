CASE ANALYSIS: CARLILL v CARBOLIC SMOKE BALL COMPANY (1893)

Citation: [1893] 1 QB 256; [1892] EWCA Civ 1. Court: Court of Appeal, England. Date of Judgment: 7 December 1892. Judges: Lindley LJ, Bowen LJ and A. L. Smith LJ. Area of Law: Law of Contract. Principal Topics: Offer and Acceptance, Unilateral Contracts, Consideration, Intention to Create Legal Relations and Advertisements.

1. Introduction

The case of Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company is one of the leading authorities in the law of contract, particularly on the principles of offer and acceptance.

The case established that an advertisement can constitute a legally binding offer where the language demonstrates a clear intention to be bound by its terms. It also established that a person can accept an offer made to the general public by performing the conditions specified in the offer without necessarily communicating acceptance to the offeror beforehand.

The decision remains an important authority in understanding unilateral contracts and the circumstances under which advertisements can create contractual obligations.

2. Facts of the Case

The defendants, Carbolic Smoke Ball Company, manufactured and sold a product known as the Carbolic Smoke Ball, which they claimed could prevent influenza and other related illnesses.

The company published an advertisement in the Pall Mall Gazette on 13 November 1891, promising to pay £100 to anyone who contracted influenza after using the smoke ball three times daily for two weeks according to the printed instructions.

The advertisement also stated that the company had deposited £1,000 with the Alliance Bank as evidence of its sincerity and commitment to paying the promised reward.

Mrs Louisa Elizabeth Carlill saw the advertisement and, relying on its representation, purchased a smoke ball.

She used the product three times daily in accordance with the instructions for several weeks. Unfortunately, despite following the instructions, she contracted influenza.

Following her illness, Mrs Carlill requested the promised £100 reward from the company.

However, the company refused to pay.

Mrs Carlill subsequently instituted an action against the company, claiming that its advertisement constituted a binding contractual offer which she had accepted by performing the stipulated conditions.

The company resisted the claim, arguing that no legally enforceable contract existed between the parties.

The matter eventually proceeded to the Court of Appeal.

3. Issues for Determination

The principal issue before the court was:

Whether the advertisement published by the Carbolic Smoke Ball Company constituted a legally binding offer capable of acceptance by Mrs Carlill.

The court also had to determine the following subsidiary issues:

1. Whether an advertisement addressed to the general public could constitute a valid offer.

2. Whether Mrs Carlill accepted the offer by using the smoke ball according to the prescribed instructions.

3. Whether communication of acceptance was necessary before the company could become contractually bound.

4. Whether there was sufficient consideration to support the company's promise.

5. Whether the company intended to create legal relations or whether the advertisement was merely a promotional statement (puff).

4. Arguments of the Parties

A. Arguments of the Plaintiff (Mrs Carlill)

Mrs Carlill argued that:

1. The company's advertisement contained a clear and definite promise to pay £100 to anyone who contracted influenza after using the smoke ball according to the specified instructions.

2. She accepted the offer by purchasing and using the smoke ball as directed.

3. She fulfilled the conditions stipulated in the advertisement but nevertheless contracted influenza.

4. The company's promise was supported by consideration because she suffered the inconvenience of using the product as instructed, while the company benefited from increased sales.

5. The statement that £1,000 had been deposited with the Alliance Bank demonstrated that the company intended its promise to be taken seriously.

Consequently, she argued that the company was contractually obligated to pay the promised reward.

B. Arguments of the Defendant (Carbolic Smoke Ball Company)

The company contended that:

1. The advertisement was not a genuine offer but merely an advertising statement intended to promote its product.

2. An offer could not legally be made to the entire world.

3. Mrs Carlill did not communicate her acceptance to the company before using the product.

4. There was no sufficient consideration to support the alleged promise.

5. The advertisement was too uncertain because it did not adequately specify the period within which a person must contract influenza to qualify for the reward.

The company therefore maintained that no enforceable contract existed.

5. Decision of the Court

The Court of Appeal unanimously dismissed the company's appeal and upheld the decision in favour of Mrs Carlill.

The court held that the advertisement constituted a valid unilateral offer made to the general public.

Mrs Carlill accepted the offer by performing the conditions stipulated in the advertisement.

The court further held that the company intended to create legal relations, particularly because it expressly stated that it had deposited £1,000 with the Alliance Bank to demonstrate its sincerity.

Accordingly, the company was legally bound to pay Mrs Carlill the promised £100 reward.

6. Reasoning of the Court

The three judges delivered separate but concurring judgments.

A. Lindley LJ

Lindley LJ held that the advertisement contained a clear promise to pay £100 to anyone who contracted influenza after using the smoke ball in the prescribed manner.

He rejected the argument that the advertisement was merely a promotional statement.

The court reasoned that the company's deposit of £1,000 with the Alliance Bank demonstrated that the promise was made seriously and was not simply advertising exaggeration.

On the issue of acceptance, Lindley LJ explained that the nature of the offer indicated that the company did not expect persons accepting it to communicate their acceptance beforehand.

Rather, acceptance would be demonstrated by performing the stipulated conditions.

He also recognised that Mrs Carlill provided consideration by undertaking the inconvenience of using the smoke ball as instructed.

B. Bowen LJ

Bowen LJ addressed the question of whether an offer could be made to the entire world.

He explained that although an offer could be addressed to the general public, it would only become a contract with those individuals who fulfilled its conditions.

He distinguished between an ordinary offer requiring communication of acceptance and a unilateral offer accepted through performance.

He also explained that consideration could consist of a benefit to the promisor or a detriment undertaken by the promisee.

In this case, the company benefited from increased sales of its product, while Mrs Carlill undertook the inconvenience of using it according to the prescribed instructions.

Consequently, sufficient consideration existed.

C. A. L. Smith LJ

A. L. Smith LJ also agreed that the advertisement constituted a valid offer.

He emphasised that the company's statement concerning the deposit of £1,000 demonstrated its intention to be legally bound.

He rejected the argument that Mrs Carlill was required to notify the company of her acceptance before performing the conditions.

Her performance of the stipulated requirements was sufficient acceptance.

7. Legal Principles Established in the Case

The case established several important principles of contract law.

A. Advertisement as an Offer

Ordinarily, advertisements are regarded as invitations to treat rather than offers.

An invitation to treat merely invites interested persons to make offers, which the advertiser may accept or reject.

However, Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company established that an advertisement can constitute a legally binding offer where its language demonstrates a clear intention to be bound upon fulfilment of specified conditions.

Application: Where a company publicly promises a reward to anyone who performs certain conditions, and the promise demonstrates contractual intention, the advertisement may constitute a valid offer.

B. Unilateral Contract

A unilateral contract arises where one party promises to do something in exchange for another party performing a specified act.

In this case, the company promised to pay £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball as directed and subsequently contracted influenza.

Mrs Carlill accepted that offer by performing the stipulated conditions.

The case therefore illustrates that a unilateral contract can arise through performance rather than an exchange of communicated promises.

C. Acceptance by Conduct

The case established that acceptance does not always require express communication to the offeror.

Where an offer expressly or impliedly contemplates acceptance through performance, the offeree may accept by performing the stipulated conditions.

Mrs Carlill was not required to write to the company announcing her intention to accept the offer before using the smoke ball.

Her conduct amounted to acceptance.

D. Consideration

Consideration is an essential element of a simple contract.

It generally refers to something of value given in exchange for a promise.

In this case, consideration existed in two respects:

1. Mrs Carlill suffered the inconvenience of using the smoke ball three times daily for two weeks.

2. The company obtained a commercial benefit through increased sales of its product.

The court therefore recognised that consideration could consist of a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee.

E. Intention to Create Legal Relations

For an agreement to become legally enforceable, the parties must intend to create legal relations.

The company argued that its advertisement was merely promotional.

However, the court rejected this argument because the company had deposited £1,000 with the Alliance Bank as evidence of its sincerity.

This demonstrated an intention to be legally bound by the promise.

F. Offer to the General Public

The case established that an offer may be made to the general public rather than to a particular individual.

However, a contract will only arise between the offeror and those persons who satisfy the stipulated conditions.

The expression "offer to the whole world" should therefore be understood in this context.

It does not mean that everyone who reads the advertisement automatically becomes a contracting party.

8. Ratio Decidendi

The ratio decidendi of the case is that:

An advertisement containing a clear and definite promise to pay a reward upon the fulfilment of specified conditions may constitute a legally binding unilateral offer, which can be accepted by performing those conditions without prior communication of acceptance where the offer does not require such notification.

Furthermore, the inconvenience undertaken by the promisee and the commercial benefit obtained by the promisor may constitute sufficient consideration.

9. Obiter Dictum

The judges made several observations concerning the general principles governing contractual offers, acceptance and consideration.

One important observation was that the law does not require every offer to be communicated to a particular individual.

An offer may be made to the public, provided its terms demonstrate an intention to be legally bound.

The judges also discussed the distinction between a genuine contractual promise and mere advertising exaggeration.

These observations help explain the reasoning behind the court's decision.

10. Significance of the Case in Nigerian Contract Law

Although Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company is an English decision, it is an important common-law authority for understanding fundamental principles of contract law in Nigeria.

The principles concerning offer, acceptance, consideration and intention to create legal relations are relevant to Nigerian contract law.

For instance, where a Nigerian company publishes an advertisement promising to reward anyone who performs certain specified conditions, the question may arise whether the advertisement constitutes a binding offer.

The reasoning in Carlill would be relevant in determining whether the company intended to create legal relations and whether the claimant accepted the offer through performance.

Practical example:

Suppose a Nigerian technology company publishes an advertisement stating:

"We will pay ₦500,000 to anyone who successfully identifies a security vulnerability in our application within 30 days, subject to the stated conditions."

If a person satisfies those conditions, the company may be contractually bound to pay the promised reward, depending on the precise terms of the offer.

The principle in Carlill would be relevant in determining the contractual obligations of the parties.

11. Other Cases to Read Alongside Carlill

The following cases provide useful comparisons when studying offer and acceptance:

Case Legal principle

Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (1953) Display of goods is generally an invitation to treat. Partridge v Crittenden (1968) Advertisements are generally invitations to treat. Fisher v Bell (1961) Display of goods in a shop window is an invitation to treat. Hyde v Wrench (1840) A counter-offer destroys the original offer. Felthouse v Bindley (1862) Silence generally does not constitute acceptance. Errington v Errington and Woods (1952) Unilateral contracts and acceptance through performance. Williams v Carwardine (1833) A person may accept a reward offer by performing its conditions.

These cases help distinguish ordinary advertisements from advertisements capable of creating contractual obligations.

12. Conclusion

Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company remains a landmark decision in the law of contract.

The case demonstrates that not every advertisement is merely an invitation to treat. Where an advertisement contains a clear promise, specifies the conditions for obtaining a reward and demonstrates an intention to create legal relations, it may constitute a binding offer.

It also establishes that acceptance of a unilateral offer may occur through performance without prior communication to the offeror.

Ultimately, the decision reinforces the principle that parties may become legally bound by promises where the essential requirements of a valid contract are satisfied.

---

Important Examination Takeaways

If you encounter this case in a contract law examination, remember these five principles:

1. Offer: An advertisement can constitute a valid offer where there is a clear intention to be bound.

2. Acceptance: Acceptance of a unilateral offer may occur through performance of its conditions.

3. Consideration: An inconvenience undertaken by the promisee may constitute sufficient consideration.

4. Intention: A deposit of money as evidence of sincerity may demonstrate an intention to create legal relations.

5. Unilateral contract: An offer may be made to the general public, but only persons who fulfil its conditions can accept it.

The most important principle to remember:

An offer does not necessarily require an express promise from the offeree in return. Where the offeror invites acceptance through the performance of a specified act, performing that act may create a binding contract.
